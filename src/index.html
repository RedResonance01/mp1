Darwin.1

A Recognition Automaton, After Edelman

This page is intended to be a student implementation of "Darwin I," a non-instructionist recognition automaton described by Gerald Edelman in Chapter 3 of Neural Darwinism. As opposed to supervised or self-supervised learning systems, the automaton relies on neither explicit instruction nor prior knowledge of the incoming stimuli in order to recognize them. The system has yet been implemented due to time constraint. The actual simulation will be implemented in the coming few days.

How Darwin I Works

  • 1. The Primary Repertoire

    A large pool of recognizer elements with a high degree of variation, here implemented as randomly generated bit strings, whose number, length and randomness are customizable. The design of the primary repertoire supposes no prior knowledge of the incoming stimuli.

  • 2. Differential Amplification of the More Adapted

    Elements within the primary repertoire whose degree of match with a stimulus exceeds some thresholds are amplified selectively by increasing their numbers and ratio within the primary repertoire. At the population level, this leads to a secondary repertoire whose responses towards experienced stimuli would be much stronger compared to the primary repertoire.

  • 3. Degeneracy Preserves Adaptive Potential

    Functional degeneracy and continuous sources of variation amongst repertoire elements ensures that the probability of recognizing novel stimuli patterns would always be positive, assuring adaptive potential of the system.

About This Simulation

In 1978, Gerald Edelman proposed the Theory of Neuronal Group Selection (labeled "Neural Darwinism"), arguing that the brain adapts through a selectional process akin to natural selection, rather than through explicit, instructive programming. "Darwin I," presented in Chapter 3 of his 1987 book Neural Darwinism, is a minimal computer automaton built to demonstrate the idea: a repertoire of variously-connected recognizer units is exposed to binary stimulus patterns, units that respond well are reinforced, and over many iterations the repertoire becomes tuned to "recognize" recurring patterns — without ever being told what those patterns are in advance.

This page implements a small interactive recreation of that experiment. The simulation engine itself is a separate, ongoing piece of work; this page focuses on presenting it.
